P1 · Primer

What happens when a model writes a token

A language model is a very large function. You give it a list of numbers, it gives back one prediction. Everything else in these notes follows from what that function is made of, and from the fact that it has to run once for every token it writes.

Why decoding is limited by memory, not arithmetic

A modern chip can do trillions of multiplications per second. A small model needs only a few billion per token. So why does a laptop write tokens at "only" 200 per second? Because the chip spends most of its time waiting for numbers to arrive from memory.

Read →P3 · Primer

What quantization is, and what Q4_0 means

Decode is limited by how many bytes it reads (primer 2). The most direct way to read fewer bytes is to store each weight in fewer bits. That is quantization.

Read →P4 · Primer

Why serving many people at once is cheap

A datacenter GPU can serve a token for a fraction of the energy a laptop needs. Most of that difference is not the chip. It is batching.

Read →P5 · Primer

How speculative decoding works

A single user cannot batch with anyone else (primer 4). Speculative decoding is a way to get some of batching's benefit anyway, by batching your own future tokens.

Read →P6 · Primer

How to measure energy without fooling yourself

Most published energy figures for AI disagree with each other, and usually not because anyone lied. They measured different things, or measured the right thing badly. These are the rules we learned, most of them by getting it wrong first.

Read →P7 · Primer

Distillation: teaching a small model to imitate a big one

Speculative decoding (primer 5) is only as good as its draft model's guesses. A draft that agrees with the big model 70% of the time saves energy; one that agrees 30% of the time wastes it (note 7). So the obvious question is: can you make a small model guess better? That is what distillation does, and it is how we built our first model family, Trace.

Read →P8 · Primer

When fewer bits pay, and when they do not

A 2-bit model reads half the bytes of a 4-bit one for every token. Reading bytes is most of what decoding costs (primer 2), so fewer bits should mean less energy per token. Our measurements say: sometimes. On a 1.5B model on a laptop chip a 2-bit token cost 13% more energy than a 4-bit one (note 20). On a 7B it cost 18% more. On a 32B it cost 10% less, and the 2-bit model won per correct answer by 16% (note 22). This primer is about why the sign flips.
