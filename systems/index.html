Systems

A local inference system, by the measurements

How to run a model well on one machine, decided by what we have measured rather than by spec sheets. This is the recipe Foundry follows and the baseline every Spliced model has to beat. Each decision cites the note that measured it. Every number is a two-pass measurement. 25 October 2026.

Summary

Run a 7B model at Q4_0, serve several requests at once, draft with a small model only where a confidence threshold keeps the guesses good, cache the prompt prefix, and cap the shader clock on machines that allow it. On the hardware we own, that recipe costs a third to a half of the naive one per correct answer. Two-bit formats pay only from about 32B up.

Decisions

Format: Q4_0, unless the model is 32B or larger. Q4_0 is the cheapest format per token at every model width we measured, on the M5 Pro, the RTX 5070 Ti Laptop GPU and the Strix Halo (notes 1, 4, 6). Below 4 bits the token gets more expensive, not cheaper, because unpacking the format costs more than the bytes it saves (note 5): on a 7B, a calibrated 2-bit token costs 18% more chip energy than a 4-bit one and Q4_0 is 21% cheaper per correct answer (note 22). At 32B the sign flips: calibrated Q2_K costs 16% less per correct answer at equal accuracy. The crossover sits between 7B and 32B on the Strix Halo and is machine dependent.

Model size: the largest that fits with room to batch. Per correct answer, a 7B serving eight requests beats a 1.5B serving one, on both machines (note 19). Accuracy buys more than speed loses. A 1.5B is the right choice only when memory forbids the 7B or when one request at a time is all there is.

Concurrency: as many requests at once as the chip rewards. Eight requests at once cut energy per token by 72% on the M5 Pro (note 1) and the second request alone saves 43% on a GPU that is already at its ceiling at batch 1 (note 10). Decode is bound by reading the weights once per step; every extra sequence in the step rides that read for free until memory or compute runs out. Where it runs out depends on the machine: on the M5 Pro a 7B is cheapest per token at 16 requests (79% less than one) and costs 15% more again at 32; on the Strix Halo energy per token keeps falling to 64, and speed per request falls in steps, so 8, 32 or 64 at once (note 24). The right number of slots is a measured optimum per machine, not "as many as fit". For a single user this means batching their own tool calls and sub-requests, not waiting for another user.

Prompt handling: cache the prefix. A prompt token costs about a fortieth of a generated one (note 18), so short prompts are nearly free. Long ones are not: at an 8,000-token prompt, prefill is 40% of a request's energy (note 18). Caching a shared prefix (a system prompt, a document) removes exactly that cost. A long context also raises the cost of every decoded token, because the key-value cache is read every step; an 8-bit cache does not win that back (notes 13, 16, 17).

Speculative decoding: on, with a confidence threshold, with the stock draft. Drafting without a threshold can be faster and more expensive at once; 16-token drafts on prose cost 3.6 to 3.95 times plain decoding (notes 7, 11). With the draft's confidence threshold at about 0.5 the energy penalty disappears on every task tested and prose gets a free 1.3 times speed-up (note 9), and the result holds in llama-server, the program deployments use (note 11). On the Strix Halo the same setting cuts chip energy per token by a third (note 21). Our own Trace v1 draft raised agreement by 0.7 points and bought nothing; the stock 0.5B draft is the one to ship until a Trace clears the 3-point bar. Drafts of up to 4 tokens are enough: at the 0.5 threshold they match drafts of 8 on prose and edits and beat them on code (1.72 against 1.51 times plain's speed, 2026-10-26 re-run of note 11's set). In llama-server the draft also needs --spec-type draft-simple; with -md alone it loads and never drafts. And only at one or two requests at once: at two it cuts energy per token 29%, and from four on, on the M5 Pro, it costs 12 to 50% more than it saves (note 25).

Clock: cap the shader clock where the platform allows it. On the Strix Halo, an 1,800 MHz cap through the OD table cuts 7B decode energy per token 37% at 4% less speed, and 42% at 8% less at 1,200 MHz (results/halo-clockcap.md). Per correct answer, under the same cap, a 7B Q4_0 at eight slots costs 28% less (111.3 against 153.8 J), a 32B Q4_0 24% less (426.2 against 563.6) and a 32B calibrated Q2_K 19% less (384.7 against 473.7), with accuracy within a point everywhere (note 23). The speed cost at eight slots is larger than the sweep's 4%: 15% on the 7B, 11% on the 32B Q4_0, 24% on the 32B Q2_K, because eight slots are closer to compute bound than one. Decode is memory bound, so the shader clock buys little speed and costs much power. Prefill is compute bound and loses a third of its speed under the cap, so a system that does long prefill should lift the cap for it. This needs root and is Linux only so far.

Measure warm. A cold first measurement understates energy per token by 20 to 30%, because the package draws about a quarter more once warm (Linux handoff, warm 126 W against cold 100 W on the 7B). Every Phase run warms up first; every number here is a warm number.

Idle is the floor. The Strix Halo idles at 17 to 26 W across an afternoon; at a fifth of the speed, under force-low, that floor is half the reading (results/halo-clockcap.md). A system that runs slowly does not save energy per token. Finish fast, then idle.

The reference configuration

Apple M5 Pro, 24 GB Qwen2.5 7B Q4_0 8 0.5B, p-min 0.5 none available 14.8 J per correct answer at 1.5B scale (note 20); 7B at 8 slots beats it (note 19)
AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 Qwen2.5 7B Q4_0 8 0.5B, p-min 0.5 OD 1,800 MHz 153.8 J per correct answer on auto (note 22); 111.3 capped (note 23)
AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 Qwen2.5 32B Q2_K, calibrated 8 none measured OD 1,800 MHz 473.7 J per correct answer on auto (note 22); 384.7 capped (note 23); Q4_0 capped is 426.2, so 2-bit's lead shrinks to 9.7% under the cap

Foundry today ships the first row without the clock cap and with the draft off by default (Fast mode turns it on). The gaps between Foundry and this table are the next things to build.

Thinking stays off by default on small models. On a 4B it cost about seven times the energy per correct answer and scored lower on maths and knowledge, because a third of its knowledge replies ran out of room (note 29). On code, a 2,048-token thinking budget that falls back to a plain answer is worth having: run live on all 164 HumanEval problems it took the 4B from 78.0% to 89.6% for 1.7 times the energy per correct answer (note 30). So thinking is set per task, with a budget, never unlimited. Letting the model answer from a cut thought, instead of starting over, scored a few points higher on code and knowledge (notes 31 and 32), but one knowledge answer in six kept reasoning until the reply limit, and it cost more. Foundry keeps the fallback.

From one machine to a cell

The same recipe scaled: six capped decode modules and one prefill module in a cell, a hundred cells in a row. The spec sheet (docs/concepts/cell-spec.md) carries every number with its basis. The modules are measured, about 1,150 tokens per second and 140 joules per correct answer on a 7B at full load including a placeholder overhead; the overhead is the prototype's first measurement. Fabric's roofline (spliced/fabric, python -m fabric.roofline) reproduces the batch floor note 8 found by hand for the published B200 server (64 against 58) and puts the chip-energy ceiling at 40 to 70% of measured wall energy, so the gap a cell has to close is named: host, supplies, fans and idle.

Not yet measured

  • The clock cap on Apple silicon and on the RTX laptop. No control is known for the first; the second has one and has not been tried.
  • Whether an 8-slot 7B beats a 1-slot 32B per correct answer on the Strix Halo. Both are measured alone; the comparison is not.
  • Speculative decoding on a model family other than Qwen2.5. The format and batching findings hold on IBM's Granite 3.3 2B (note 28).
  • A budget on the answer as well as the thought, so a cut thought cannot run on in the reply (note 32).
